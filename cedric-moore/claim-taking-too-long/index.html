My Insurance Claim Is Taking Forever. What Can I Do?

Approved by Cedric Moore, licensed public adjuster serving Orlando, FL.

Call CedricText Cedric a photo
THE SHORT ANSWER

Florida law sets timeframes for property claims: the insurer generally must acknowledge your claim within 7 days and pay or deny it, or the undisputed part of it, within 60 days of notice, with limited exceptions. If your claim is stalled, ask in writing for a status update and a copy of the adjuster's estimate, keep a log, and know that free state mediation is available for many disputes. How long ago did you report your claim?

Waiting on an insurance company while you live in a damaged house wears you down. Some delays have real reasons, like a backlog after a big storm, but Florida law sets clear timeframes for insurers, and knowing them gives you something firm to point to. Do you know the date you first reported the claim?

Florida generally requires your insurer to acknowledge a claim communication within 7 days and to pay or deny the claim, or the part of it they agree on, within 60 days after they receive notice. There are limited exceptions, like factors beyond the insurer's control, but a claim that sits for months with no answer is worth questioning. Has it been more than 60 days since you reported?

Put your questions in writing. A phone call leaves no record, and adjusters change, but an email asking for the claim status, the adjuster's name and a copy of the detailed estimate creates a paper trail the insurer has to respond to. Have you received a copy of the insurer's estimate yet?

Delays sometimes come from missing paperwork. Insurers can pause certain timeframes while they wait on information they asked you for, so a quick check of what they requested and when you sent it often finds the hold-up, but if you've sent everything, write that down and ask what else they need. Did the insurer ask you for documents or an inspection?

If the claim is stuck or you disagree with the outcome, you have options. Florida offers free mediation through the Department of Financial Services for many residential property disputes, paid for by the insurer, and a licensed public adjuster can push the claim forward on your behalf. Would you like help figuring out which step makes sense for your claim?

What to do next

  1. Write down the date you first reported the claim.
  2. Email the insurer for a status update, the adjuster's name and the detailed estimate.
  3. Keep a log of every call, email and letter with dates.
  4. Check whether the insurer is waiting on anything from you.
  5. Ask about free state mediation, or have a licensed public adjuster step in.

Every week a claim drags on is another week living with damage. Delays are common, but they don't have to be endless, and a clear written record is what gets a stuck claim moving again. Ready to call or text Cedric so we can look at where your claim stands?

Questions homeowners ask

Why is my first insurance check way smaller than my roofer's estimate?

Insurance companies usually pay claims in two parts, starting with the depreciated value of your damaged property rather than the full replacement cost. They can also leave out local building code upgrades, labor rates, or hidden water damage that a contractor sees right away. But you may be able to recover the held-back money and add missed items once the full scope of repairs is properly documented and submitted.

I review your insurer's payout sheet side by side with real local repair costs to find what they left out.

The adjuster only spent ten minutes at my house and looked at one area. Is that normal?

Field adjusters often carry heavy caseloads and may only have instructions from the desk adjuster to look at one specific area. When they rush through and skip the attic, walls, floors or ceilings, hidden water and other damage easily go unrecorded. But an incomplete inspection does not close the door on your claim when you bring in thorough photo and moisture documentation.

Should I cash the first check the insurance company sent me?

Many homeowners freeze when a check arrives because they worry that depositing it means the claim is finished forever. In many cases, cashing an initial undisputed payment does not stop you from asking for the rest of what you are owed, but always check the memo line and the letter that came with it to make sure it does not say "full and final settlement."

I look at the paperwork that came with your check before you endorse it, so you know what rights you keep.

Insurers frequently blame roof leaks, pipe leaks and water damage on age, wear and tear, or a leak they say went on too long. A denial letter feels like the final word, but sudden damage leaves different signs than slow aging when it's documented correctly.

How much does it cost to hire a public adjuster in Florida?

Public adjusters usually work on a contingency fee, which means you pay nothing upfront and pay a percentage of the claim money recovered. Fees vary by claim and by whether the state has declared an emergency, but Florida law caps what we can charge and requires that you get the fee in writing before signing any contract.

I go over my written fee agreement line by line with you first so there are no surprises.

Florida homeowners worry about losing coverage in a tough insurance market, and carriers do adjust rates after heavy loss years. Rate and renewal decisions depend on your insurer and your policy, but hiring a licensed public adjuster is your right, and your agent can tell you how a claim affects your policy.

Should I make repairs before the insurance adjuster comes to look at my house?

You have a duty under your policy to protect your home from further damage, like tarping a leaking roof or extracting standing water. Tearing out drywall or replacing a roof before the insurer sees it can hurt your claim, but clear photos and videos taken before emergency dry-out protect both your house and your claim.

I tell you what temporary steps to take right now and how to document everything before permanent work starts.

What is "recoverable depreciation" and how do I get it back?

When an insurer calculates your loss, it subtracts a percentage for the age and condition of your roof or flooring and holds that money back until the work is done. That held-back amount is called recoverable depreciation, but if you have a Replacement Cost Value policy, you can claim it once you show proof that repairs are underway or done.

I track your depreciation holdbacks and submit the right invoices so the carrier releases the rest.

Can't my roofer or general contractor just handle the insurance claim for me?

Good contractors know how to rebuild your house and can give you a fair price for their trade. Under Florida law, a contractor cannot negotiate your insurance settlement unless they also hold a public adjuster license, but working alongside a public adjuster lets your contractor focus on building while I handle the policy and pricing.

I coordinate directly with your contractor so the approved scope matches what it takes to fix your home.

How long do I have to file or reopen a claim in Florida?

Florida has strict deadlines for reporting a new claim and shorter windows for a supplemental claim when hidden damage shows up later. Waiting too long can bar you from collecting money you are owed, but acting quickly preserves your right to reopen an underpaid file.

I check your date of loss against current Florida deadlines right away.
