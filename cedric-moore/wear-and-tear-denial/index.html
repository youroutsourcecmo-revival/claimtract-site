My Claim Was Denied for Wear and Tear. Now What?

Approved by Cedric Moore, Licensed Public Adjuster · Orlando, FL

THE SHORT ANSWER

A denial letter blaming "wear and tear," age or a "long-term leak" is one of the most common answers Florida homeowners get, on roofs, plumbing and water damage alike. Insurance companies sometimes blame age when the real damage is subtle, but a denial letter is not the final word if a proper inspection shows something sudden caused it. Did your denial letter say your home simply wore out?

Getting a denial letter after years of paying premiums on time feels like a slap in the face. The letter usually quotes policy exclusions and says the damage is normal aging or a slow leak, but sudden damage and long-term wear leave different signs when someone knows what to look for. Would you like to know how the two are told apart?

Every home in Orlando ages, and older roofs, pipes and fixtures show wear. Sudden damage looks different: a roof lifts and creases in a wind event, a supply line bursts, a drain backs up, and water spreads in a pattern that slow seepage doesn't make, but a rushed inspection can miss those signs until someone documents them carefully. Did the adjuster explain exactly what they saw that made them call it wear and tear?

Another reason insurers deny older homes is that old materials break when someone tries to repair them. When a repair attempt damages the surrounding area, building rules can require replacing more than a patch, but carriers sometimes call that brittleness wear and tear. Has a contractor told you the damaged area is too old to patch?

Many homeowners put the denial letter in a drawer and start looking at loans to pay for repairs themselves. Walking away without a second opinion means paying for it all yourself, but having a licensed public adjuster re-inspect the damage and respond with clear evidence can give the carrier a reason to take a second look. Are you about to pay out of pocket just because the first letter said no?

Time matters once you get a denial, because the damage can keep getting worse and Florida sets deadlines for disputing a claim decision. Leaving it alone can lead to new damage the insurer may blame on neglect, but acting quickly lets you protect your home and dispute the decision while the evidence is still fresh. When did you get your denial letter, and have you stopped the damage from spreading?

What to do next

  1. Save the denial letter and its envelope, and write down the date you received it.
  2. Ask your insurance company for the field adjuster's photos and inspection report.
  3. Stop the damage from spreading, like a tarp, a shut-off valve or drying fans, and keep every receipt.
  4. Don't let anyone tear out the damaged area before you get a second inspection.
  5. Have a licensed public adjuster inspect the damage for its real cause.

Many homeowners give up the moment they read a denial letter. You don't have to take one adjuster's opinion as the final truth when your own eyes tell you something sudden happened, but you need clear, documented proof to make the carrier take a second look.
