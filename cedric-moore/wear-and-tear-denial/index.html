My Roof Claim Was Denied for Wear and Tear. Now What?

Approved by Cedric Moore, Licensed Public Adjuster · Orlando, FL

THE SHORT ANSWER

A denial letter blaming "wear and tear" is one of the most common responses Florida homeowners get, especially on roofs over ten years old. Insurance companies sometimes blame age when wind damage is subtle, but a denial letter is not the final word if a proper inspection shows a recent storm damaged your roof. Did your denial letter say your roof simply died of old age?

Getting a denial letter after years of paying premiums on time feels like a slap in the face. The letter usually quotes policy exclusions and says your leaks or missing shingles are normal aging, but subtle wind creases and lifted seals can be mistaken for ordinary wear and tear. Would you like to know the difference between an old roof and a storm-damaged roof?

Every roof in Orlando takes a beating from the Florida sun, and older shingles lose granules over time. High winds leave specific damage: shingles lift, bend back, crease across the top and lose their seal so rain blows underneath, but a quick look from the ground or a rushed photo report can miss broken seals until someone checks the shingles by hand. Did the adjuster actually check whether your shingles were unsealed or creased?

Another reason insurers deny older roofs is that brittle shingles crack when a roofer lifts them to slide in a replacement. When a repair attempt breaks the surrounding shingles, building rules can require replacing more than a patch, but carriers sometimes call that brittleness wear and tear. Has a local roofer told you your roof is too brittle to patch?

Many homeowners put the denial letter in a drawer and start looking at loans to pay for a new roof themselves. Walking away without a second opinion means paying for it all yourself, but having a licensed public adjuster re-inspect the roof and respond with clear photo evidence can give the carrier a reason to take a second look. Are you about to pay out of pocket just because the first letter said no?

Time matters once you get a denial, because your roof is still exposed to the next afternoon storm and Florida sets deadlines for disputing a claim decision. Leaving damaged shingles alone can lead to new leaks the insurer may blame on neglect, but acting quickly lets you protect your home and dispute the decision while the storm evidence is still fresh. When did you get your denial letter, and have you kept the water out since?

What to do next

  1. Save the denial letter and its envelope, and write down the date you received it.
  2. Ask your insurance company for the field adjuster's photos and inspection report.
  3. Tarp any active leaks right away and keep the receipt for the tarp and labor.
  4. Don't let anyone tear off the roof before you get a second inspection.
  5. Have a licensed public adjuster inspect the roof for wind creases, unsealed tabs and storm patterns.

Many homeowners give up the moment they read a denial letter. You don't have to take one adjuster's opinion as the final truth when your own eyes tell you the roof was fine before the storm hit Orlando, but you need clear, documented proof to make the carrier take a second look.
