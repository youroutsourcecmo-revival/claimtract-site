Public Adjuster vs. Insurance Adjuster: Who Works for You on a Homeowners Insurance Claim, and Who Doesn't?

Orlando, FL · Approved by Cedric Moore, Licensed Public Adjuster

The inspector has been at your house. He looked at the stained ceiling, took a few pictures, and left. But the water reached the bedroom next door. Nobody looked inside that room. Nobody measured the damaged floor. A few days later, an estimate arrives. The room you were worried about is not listed.

Who decides what makes it into that estimate? Is it the person who visited your home, someone reviewing the file at a desk, or someone higher up?

That is where many homeowners get confused. The person you meet may not be the person reviewing the paperwork. And neither person works for you simply because they are handling your claim. Understanding who does what can help you ask better questions, keep better records, and know when you may need someone representing your side.

The moment your claim gets a number

When you report damage, the insurance company opens a claim and gives it a claim number. That number becomes the reference for the paperwork, calls, inspection, and later decisions. The company may assign several people to handle different parts of the file. You might meet one person at your home and never speak directly with the others.

The important distinction is who each person works for. A company staff adjuster is employed by the insurance company. An independent adjuster is hired by the company on a contract basis. Both work for the insurance company. A public adjuster is different. You hire that licensed insurance professional to represent you, the policyholder. A public adjuster documents damage, reads your policy, prepares the claim, and negotiates with the insurance company on your behalf.

Those are different responsibilities. A person inspecting your home for the insurance company is not your personal representative. You can ask anyone handling the claim who they work for. In Florida, public adjusters must hold a state license. You can check a license through the Florida Department of Financial Services.

The field adjuster: the person who visits your home

The field adjuster is often the person you meet in person. This person visits the property, looks at the reported damage, takes photographs, and gathers information for the claim. That visit can feel like the most important moment in the process. But the inspection is only one part of the claim. The written estimate and later review may involve people you never meet.

A field adjuster may be an independent contractor hired to inspect a property. That person may work with several insurance companies. The field adjuster is usually gathering information, not making the final payment decision. Pay attention to what gets inspected. Did the person look at every affected room? Were the floors, walls, ceilings, and damaged belongings included? Did the inspector take photographs of the areas you pointed out?

Sometimes the inspection focuses on the area first reported. A roof inspection, for example, may not automatically include a room where water later appeared. Water damage can extend beyond the first visible stain. You should explain what happened and point out every area you believe was affected. You can also ask whether those areas will be included in the inspection report.

Take your own photographs and video when it is safe. Start with the full room, then photograph the details. Keep damaged materials when practical, or photograph them before they are removed. If you discover damage after the inspection, document it. You can ask the insurance company how to submit the additional photographs and information or request another inspection.

The key is to compare what the inspector saw with what actually happened in your home. A visit does not help much if important damage never makes it into the record.

The desk adjuster: the person reviewing the paperwork

You may never meet the desk adjuster. This person usually works from the claim file rather than visiting your property. The desk adjuster reviews the claim notes and inspection information. The desk adjuster may also give the field adjuster instructions about what to inspect before the visit. That matters because the first report of damage can shape what gets looked at. If the original report mentions one room or one part of the roof, the inspection may focus there. Damage outside that area can be missed unless someone brings it forward.

After the inspection, the desk adjuster commonly reviews the report and estimate. That review helps determine what damage and repair work appear in the insurance company's evaluation. You should not assume that every item you mentioned during the inspection appears in the final estimate. Read the estimate and compare it with your own photographs and records.

Look at the rooms and areas listed. Check the repair work described. Compare the measurements and damaged materials with what you can see in your home. If something is missing, ask for an explanation. Send photographs, contractor findings, receipts, or other records that help document the issue. Keep a copy of what you send and note when you sent it.

The desk adjuster may be working with information gathered by someone else. Your job is to make sure the file reflects the damage you are reporting. A clear written record gives the reviewer something specific to consider.

The supervisor: when the file moves higher

A desk adjuster may have limits on the amount of authority available for a claim. When a claim involves a larger amount or a decision outside that adjuster's authority, a supervisor may become involved. The supervisor is a manager who may review the claim and the decisions made below that level. Supervisors also watch claim figures and the amounts being paid across the work they oversee.

This can affect what happens when you disagree with an estimate. The desk adjuster may not have authority to approve every requested amount. The file may need another level of review. That does not mean a supervisor will automatically change the estimate. It means the homeowner should understand whether the issue has been reviewed and what explanation supports the decision.

A detailed estimate can make that conversation more useful. It should identify the damaged areas, the work needed, and the amounts being requested. Photographs and other records can help support those details. If you are told the claim is waiting for approval, ask what is being reviewed. Ask whether the reviewer needs more information. If a decision has been made, request the explanation in writing.

You do not need to guess how much authority someone has. Ask whether the person reviewing the file can address the issue you raised. Keep the answer with your claim documents.

The legal department: when the disagreement becomes a legal matter

The legal department is another layer a homeowner may hear about, especially when a claim becomes disputed. Most claims do not begin with a lawyer handling the homeowner's everyday questions. The field adjuster inspects. The desk adjuster reviews the file. A supervisor may review decisions that need another level of approval.

Legal staff may become involved when a dispute develops into a legal issue. Insurance attorneys handle legal disputes and lawsuits. Their role is different from the person who inspects the property or reviews the estimate. A legal referral does not, by itself, tell you what the final outcome will be. You need to read the actual letter or communication and understand what it says.

If you receive a denial or a letter explaining the insurer's position, keep the entire document. Look for the reason given and any policy language identified. Ask for an explanation in writing if you do not understand the decision. A reservation-of-rights letter is one document worth understanding. It means the insurance company is still reviewing coverage. It is not the same as a denial, but it deserves attention.

You should not ignore a legal or policy letter because the wording is difficult. Keep it with your claim records and get help reviewing what it says. If you need legal advice, speak with a qualified attorney.

What you can do at every stage

You cannot control how the insurance company assigns its employees or contractors. But you can control how carefully you document your own claim. Start with the damage. Take photographs and video before cleanup when it is safe. Keep receipts for emergency work and save copies of estimates and reports.

A short inspection can leave questions. But your own photographs and records can help show what was there and what changed. Keep every letter, email, estimate, and receipt together. Write down the claim number and the dates of important calls. Note who you spoke with and what was discussed. When an estimate arrives, compare it with the damage you documented. If a room or item is missing, ask why. If the answer is unclear, request it in writing.

A denial or low estimate does not mean you should stop asking questions. You can ask for the reason, review the policy language, and provide additional documentation. There may be another inspection or another level of review. A public adjuster can review the policy, document the damage, prepare the claim, and negotiate with the insurance company on your behalf.

Layer One: The Field Adjuster

The field adjuster is the person who shows up at your front door with a camera, a ladder, or a moisture meter. Sometimes this person is a company staff adjuster employed directly by the insurance company. Often, though, they are an independent adjuster hired on a contract basis who may handle claims for several different insurance companies at once.

Whether staff or independent, the field adjuster works for the insurance company, not for you. They are usually friendly and polite while measuring and taking photos of the area they were told to inspect. Yet in many cases, the field adjuster does not have the authority to decide what your claim pays.

Their job is to gather information and send a report back to the office. During a quick ten-minute walkthrough focused on one roof slope or a single stained ceiling, wet insulation, swollen baseboards, and attic damage easily go unrecorded. Taking your own wide and close-up photos before cleanup—and saving damaged parts like a cut pipe or torn drywall—gives you proof even when that first visit is brief.

Layer Two: The Desk Adjuster

The desk adjuster is the person handling your claim file from an office you never visit. They read your initial claim notes first and frequently tell the field adjuster which specific area to inspect. If your first phone call only mentioned the roof or one wet room, the inspection may be set up for that single spot alone.

Because the desk adjuster never steps foot in your house, they only know what is written in the file. They review the field adjuster's photos, apply your policy rules and deductible—including separate Florida hurricane deductibles of 2%, 5% or 10% of the home's insured value—and build or approve the repair estimate. Still, an incomplete report does not close the door on recovering the rest of your loss.

A desk file moves when concrete proof replaces guesswork. Submitting clean room-by-room photos, moisture checks, and local repair pricing gives the office adjuster a complete record to evaluate. Every letter, estimate, and photo set you send becomes part of the permanent file that desk adjuster must review.

Layer Three: The Supervisor

Above the desk adjuster sits a supervisor or manager who oversees claim files and payout limits. A desk adjuster's authority can stop at a set dollar limit, meaning larger repairs or disputed items have to go higher up for review. Even when a field adjuster sees major damage, the final check may wait on sign-off from that next level.

Homeowners rarely speak directly with the supervisor reviewing their file. Your claim can sit waiting in review while a manager checks total payout numbers, repair scope, and policy limits. When a file comes back from that level, homeowners often see cuts to local labor rates, material quantities, or building code upgrades. Yet a supervisor reviews files based on policy language and documented proof, not phone calls asking for a favor.

Larger residential claims often stall at this stage because key construction steps were left out of the original notes. For example, an initial estimate might pay to patch one small section of a roof or paint a single wall without accounting for brittle older materials or matching rules across an open room. Supervisors look at whether the file proves why a full repair is required rather than a spot patch.

A documented, line-by-line repair estimate backed by clear photos and your policy's declarations page gives a supervisor concrete facts to review. Instead of arguing over opinions, the file shows exact measurements, local repair bids, and the exact endorsements or exclusions that apply. Having a licensed representative who knows how to organize that proof in the format a supervisor expects keeps the review focused on the facts of the loss.

Layer Four: The Legal Department

In the background of every insurance company sits the legal department, along with outside insurance attorneys. As long as a homeowner accepts the first check and nobody questions a low estimate or a partial denial, lawyers never have to step into the file. When a dispute drags on, a claim grows complex, or a carrier issues a formal coverage denial, legal staff may review the letters and policy exclusions.

Many homeowners see a formal letter quoting dense policy sections—or a "reservation of rights" letter saying coverage is still under review—and assume the claim is finished. That reaction is especially common when a letter blames roof or water damage on "wear and tear," "old age," or a "long-term leak". Families often worry that questioning a denial or low offer means going to court or paying legal fees out of pocket. However, pushing back on an incomplete estimate or a disputed denial does not automatically mean a lawsuit.

Sudden damage—such as wind-creased shingles or a burst supply line—frequently gets mislabeled as normal aging during a brief initial walkthrough. Before a dispute ever reaches a courtroom, homeowners have structured options built into their policies and Florida rules. Many policies include an appraisal process specifically for resolving disputes over the dollar amount of a loss, and Florida offers a free mediation program through the Department of Financial Services for many residential property disputes.

Even when the carrier asks for a sworn proof of loss—a formal statement of the amount being claimed—the dispute is still about documenting the facts and the policy language. A licensed public adjuster can inspect the real cause of the damage, prepare the supporting documentation, and negotiate the repair numbers directly with the carrier. If a legal dispute or lawsuit eventually becomes necessary, an insurance attorney handles that stage, while a public adjuster focuses on valuing and negotiating the claim itself.

Who Works for You—and What to Do at Each Layer

Four layers work for the insurance company, while a public adjuster works strictly for you. In Florida, a public adjuster is licensed by the state and verified through the Department of Financial Services to represent the policyholder. Unlike a contractor—who rebuilds your home but cannot legally negotiate your insurance settlement in Florida without a public adjuster license—a public adjuster reads your policy, documents every room, and handles the back-and-forth with the carrier.

Most public adjusters work on a contingency fee, meaning you pay nothing up front and pay a percentage of the claim settlement. Florida law caps how much a public adjuster can charge, with lower limits after a declared emergency, and requires the fee to be explained in a written contract before you sign. Going through that written agreement line by line before signing keeps every fee and responsibility clear from day one. Whether you hire representation right away or after a low first check, you can take clear steps at every stage of the claim:

  • Before the field adjuster arrives: Protect the home from further damage by shutting off water or tarping a roof, take wide and close-up photos first, and keep damaged materials and emergency receipts.
  • When the desk adjuster sends an estimate: Print the full Statement of Loss, check whether depreciation was held back under an Actual Cash Value calculation, and read the memo line and back of any check before depositing it to make sure it does not say "full and final settlement".
  • If the file stalls at a supervisor or gets denied: Ask for the reason and cited policy section in writing, request the field adjuster's photos and inspection report, and have a licensed public adjuster inspect the property for hidden or sudden damage before tearing anything out.

The Riveras could make a room-by-room list and photograph each visible area. They could keep wide photographs showing where each room sits in the house, then take close-ups of the damage. If they had receipts for emergency work, those belonged with the claim records too. There is another important distinction. A field adjuster's inspection is not the same as a complete repair plan. The estimate reflects the information and scope included in the claim review. If the hallway damage is missing, the Riveras can ask how to submit photographs and other documentation for that area.

The desk adjuster they never meet

After the field visit, the claim may move to a desk adjuster. This person reviews information in the file rather than inspecting the home in person. A desk adjuster may review photographs, inspection notes, estimates, policy information, and other documents. The desk adjuster may also ask for more information or communicate what the file still needs. The homeowner may never see this person.

That is what happened next for the Riveras. Their paperwork showed the living-room stain, but the hallway was still missing. The desk review could only consider the information that reached the file. This is where a homeowner's own records become important. A photograph sitting on a phone does not help the desk review unless someone submits it. The Riveras needed to send the hallway photographs and identify the room clearly.

They could also ask for the field inspection report and the line-by-line estimate. Those documents would help them compare what the inspector recorded with what the estimate included. If the documents left out a room, they could point to the specific omission. A missing item does not automatically mean the insurer must pay for it. The policy and the facts still matter. But a clear record gives the desk adjuster something specific to review instead of a general statement that the estimate is too low.

When the file reaches a supervisor

A supervisor may become involved when a claim needs review beyond the authority of the person handling it. The exact process differs by company and claim. A desk adjuster may have limits on what they can approve. A question about a larger estimate, a disputed scope, or a decision needing additional review may be referred higher. That does not mean every claim follows the same approval chain.

The Riveras sent photographs of the hallway and asked for the estimate to be reviewed. The file did not change right away. They were left waiting, without knowing whether the added information had been considered. Silence does not tell a homeowner what is happening inside a claim file. It is better to ask a clear question than to guess. The Riveras could ask whether the new photographs had been received, whether the hallway was included, and whether anything else was needed.

They could keep a simple contact record: the date they sent the documents, who received them, and what response came back. That record would help them explain the history if another person took over the file. A supervisor's review is still part of the insurance company's process. It does not make the supervisor the homeowner's representative. If the homeowner wants someone working on their side, that is a separate role.

The letter that sounded final

The next document can feel more final than it really is. A letter may explain that the insurer has made a decision, limited payment, or still needs to review coverage. The Riveras received a letter that did not include everything they expected. They had to work out whether it was a denial, a partial decision, or a reservation of rights.

A reservation of rights letter means the insurer is still reviewing coverage. It is not the same as a denial. The wording matters, so the Riveras needed to read the whole letter rather than rely on its most alarming sentence. If the insurer denied part of the claim or made a low offer, the homeowners could ask for the reason in writing. They could also read the policy section the letter cited and compare it with the damage records. A reinspection, added documents, or another estimate may help clarify a disagreement.

Some claim disputes involve the amount of damage or the cost of repairs. A policy may contain an appraisal process for disputes about the amount. Florida also has a Department of Financial Services mediation program for eligible residential property disputes. Mediation is not a court hearing, and it does not decide every kind of dispute.

The insurer may also involve its legal department when a claim raises legal questions or a dispute needs legal review. That does not mean the homeowner is automatically going to court. Disagreeing with an estimate, asking for a reinspection, or submitting more documentation does not by itself mean a lawsuit is coming.

The Riveras could start with the documents in front of them. They could identify what the letter decided, what it left open, and what question they wanted answered. If they did not understand the wording, they could ask a qualified professional to review the letter and policy with them.

The first check is smaller than the contractor's estimate

When the first check arrived, the Riveras compared it with the contractor's estimate. The numbers did not match. That difference did not explain itself. One reason may be depreciation. Actual cash value, or ACV, generally means repair or replacement cost minus depreciation for age and wear. Replacement cost value, or RCV, means the cost to repair or replace without subtracting depreciation.

Some policies pay ACV first and hold back recoverable depreciation until repairs are completed. That can make the first payment smaller than the full repair estimate. The Riveras needed to look at the payment breakdown and policy, not guess from the check amount alone. Other differences may come from the scope of work. The insurer's estimate may list fewer repairs than the contractor's estimate. The deductible also affects the amount paid. The Riveras could compare the estimates line by line and mark what appeared in one but not the other.

Florida homeowners should also check the declarations page for the hurricane deductible. Florida law generally requires insurers to offer certain hurricane deductible choices, including 2%, 5%, and 10% of the policy dwelling limits, subject to statutory exceptions. The actual dollar amount for the policy's hurricane deductible should appear on the declarations page.

A percentage deductible can be much larger than a flat-dollar deductible. It is not calculated as a percentage of the repair estimate. The Riveras needed to find the actual dollar amount shown in their own policy documents and understand whether it applied to their loss. The check and its accompanying letter also deserve a careful read. If either uses wording such as "full and final settlement," the Riveras should not guess what signing or endorsing it means. They could ask a qualified professional to review the exact wording before deciding what to do.

Their next step was to gather the insurer's estimate, the contractor's estimate, the declarations page, and the letter that came with the check. That gave someone reviewing the claim a way to compare the numbers and identify the actual points of disagreement.

Who works for the homeowner?

The people handling the insurer's side of the claim have different jobs. The field adjuster inspects. The desk adjuster reviews the file. A supervisor may review matters that need another level of authority. The legal department may become involved when legal review is needed. None of those roles is the same as a public adjuster hired by the homeowner. A public adjuster is a licensed insurance professional who works for the policyholder, not the insurance company.

A public adjuster can document damage, review the policy, prepare the claim, and negotiate with the insurer on the homeowner's behalf. The work may include inspecting the property, taking photographs and measurements, reviewing letters, and preparing an estimate. For the Riveras, that would mean having someone examine the missing hallway damage from their side of the claim. It would also mean comparing the insurer's estimate with the contractor's estimate and reviewing the policy language that applies. The goal is to present the claim with supporting information, not promise a particular payment.

In Florida, public adjusters must be licensed by the state. Homeowners can check a license through the Florida Department of Financial Services. A person offering to adjust or negotiate a property claim for compensation should not be treated as a public adjuster simply because they use that title. A public adjuster's fee should be explained in a written contract before the homeowner signs. Florida law places limits on public adjuster compensation, and the rules can depend on the type of claim and circumstances. The homeowner should read the agreement, understand how the fee is calculated, and ask questions before signing.

A contractor has a different job. Contractors estimate and perform repair work. A contractor's estimate may help document the cost of repairs, but that does not make the contractor the homeowner's public adjuster. The Riveras did not need to guess which person was on which side. They could check the license, read the contract, and ask exactly what services the professional would provide. The important question was not just who had a title, but who had been hired to represent them.

Back at the kitchen table

The Riveras spread the paperwork across the kitchen table. The claim number was on the folder. The photographs showed the living room and hallway. The estimates showed different scopes. The letter explained the insurer's position, but some questions remained. They could not decide the claim by looking at one number. They needed to understand what had been inspected, what had been included, what the policy said, and what the insurer's letter actually decided.

That is the work a careful claim review begins with. Put the documents together, identify what is missing, and ask specific questions. If the answers depend on the policy or the damage itself, have someone qualified review those materials. When you look at your own claim file tonight, can you tell who inspected your home, who reviewed the estimate, what the insurer decided, and who is working for you?
