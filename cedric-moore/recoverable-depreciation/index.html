What Is Recoverable Depreciation and How Do I Get It Back?

Approved by Cedric Moore, licensed public adjuster serving Orlando, FL.

Call CedricText Cedric a photo
THE SHORT ANSWER

On a replacement cost policy, the insurer often holds back depreciation from the first check and pays it later, once the repair or replacement is done. That held-back amount is called recoverable depreciation, and you usually get it by completing the work and sending proof, such as the final invoice, within the time your policy allows. Does your estimate show a line for depreciation being held back?

Most homeowners are surprised when the first check is smaller than the estimate the insurer itself wrote. The missing money often isn't denied at all, it's held back as depreciation, but you have to know it's there and ask for it the right way to see it. Have you looked at the totals page of your insurance estimate?

Depreciation is the insurer's way of accounting for the age and wear of what was damaged. On an actual cash value basis, they subtract it, but if you have replacement cost coverage, much of that amount is recoverable, meaning they pay it once you actually repair or replace the item. Do you know whether your policy is replacement cost or actual cash value?

The estimate usually shows it plainly if you know where to look. You'll see a replacement cost total, a depreciation amount and an actual cash value total, and the first check is often based on the actual cash value minus your deductible, but the difference between the two is money waiting for you if it's marked recoverable. Can you find the word recoverable anywhere on your estimate?

Getting it back takes paperwork. Most policies ask for proof the work was done, like a signed contract and a final invoice, and many set a deadline for completing repairs or submitting proof, but if you keep your contractor's paperwork organized, the request is usually straightforward. Has your contractor given you a written contract with a price?

The trap is when the estimate itself is too low. If the insurer missed damage or underpriced the work, the depreciation they hold back is also too low, but a full, accurate estimate raises both what you're paid now and what you can recover later. Would you like a second set of eyes on your estimate before you start repairs?

What to do next

  1. Find the replacement cost, depreciation and actual cash value lines on your estimate.
  2. Check your policy for replacement cost coverage and any deadline to complete repairs.
  3. Get a written contract and keep the final invoice from your contractor.
  4. Send proof of completion to the insurer and keep a copy.
  5. Have a licensed public adjuster check whether the original estimate missed damage.

It's frustrating to fix your home and still feel like part of the money never came. Recoverable depreciation goes unclaimed more often than it should, but you can track it from the first estimate and get it back with the right paperwork. Insurance companies usually pay claims in two parts, starting with the depreciated value of your damaged property rather than the full replacement cost. But you may be able to recover the held-back money and add missed items once the full scope of repairs is properly documented and submitted.

How much does it cost to hire a public adjuster in Florida?

Public adjusters usually work on a contingency fee, which means you pay nothing upfront and pay a percentage of the claim money recovered. Fees vary by claim and by whether the state has declared an emergency, but Florida law caps what we can charge and requires that you get the fee in writing before signing any contract.

Should I make repairs before the insurance adjuster comes to look at my house?

You have a duty under your policy to protect your home from further damage, like tarping a leaking roof or extracting standing water. Tearing out drywall or replacing a roof before the insurer sees it can hurt your claim, but clear photos and videos taken before emergency dry-out protect both your house and your claim.

What is "recoverable depreciation" and how do I get it back?

When an insurer calculates your loss, it subtracts a percentage for the age and condition of your roof or flooring and holds that money back until the work is done. That held-back amount is called recoverable depreciation, but if you have a Replacement Cost Value policy, you can claim it once you show proof that repairs are underway or done.

I track your depreciation holdbacks and submit the right invoices so the carrier releases the rest.

Good contractors know how to rebuild your house and can give you a fair price for their trade.
