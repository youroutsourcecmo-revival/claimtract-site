Why Is My First Insurance Check Smaller Than My Estimate?

Approved by Cedric Moore, Licensed Public Adjuster · Orlando, FL

THE SHORT ANSWER

Your first insurance check is usually smaller because the carrier subtracts depreciation for the age of your home and can leave out hidden repairs or local code upgrades. Under a replacement cost policy, this first check is rarely the final amount owed, but you have to document the missing items and request the rest properly. Have you looked at the line-by-line breakdown that came with your payment yet?

Opening an envelope from your insurance company and seeing a check for half of what your contractor quoted is a sinking feeling. Most homeowners assume the insurer simply refused to pay for their roof or interior repairs, but the gap usually comes down to how insurance math works and what the first inspector left off the list. Would you like to know the reasons that check came up short?

The biggest reason is called depreciation, which is how insurers reduce the value of your shingles, drywall or flooring based on how old they are. If your roof is twelve years old, the company may hold back thousands of dollars from the first check until you prove the repairs are being made, but if you have a Replacement Cost Value policy, that held-back money still belongs to you once the proper paperwork is filed. Do you know whether your policy pays Replacement Cost or Actual Cash Value?

The second reason is that the first scope of work often misses real construction steps. An estimate may only pay to replace a small patch of shingles or paint one wall, ignoring that brittle shingles break when lifted or paint won't match across an open room, but showing why a partial repair won't work makes the carrier look at the full repair area. Did your insurer's estimate only pay to patch a small section?

The third reason is hidden damage that nobody looked for during a quick walkthrough. Water travels behind baseboards, under laminate flooring and through attic insulation long before a brown stain shows on your ceiling, but a thorough moisture check and full inspection can bring those unseen repairs into your claim. Has anyone checked behind your walls or inside your attic since the storm?

Many people also forget the hurricane or all-peril deductible when they look at the bottom line. In Florida, hurricane deductibles are often two to five percent of your home's insured value, which takes a big bite out of the first payout, but when the total damage is undercounted, your deductible can eat almost the whole check. Is your deductible taking up most of the money they sent you?

Finally, estimating software can use pricing that doesn't reflect what licensed tradespeople in Orlando actually charge after a major storm. Labor and material costs jump when everyone in town needs a roof at the same time, but accurate local repair bids and code requirements help bring the numbers closer to reality. Have you compared the prices on your insurance sheet against a real local bid?

What to do next

  1. Print the full Statement of Loss and repair estimate, not just the cover letter with the check.
  2. Ask a licensed local contractor for a detailed, room-by-room estimate to compare against the insurer's list.
  3. Find the column labeled "Depreciation" to see how much money is being held back.
  4. Photograph any cracked tiles, lifted shingles or interior water spots that aren't on the adjuster's report.
  5. Call a licensed public adjuster to review both estimates side by side before you sign repair contracts.

You should not have to drain your savings to cover repairs your insurance policy was supposed to pay for. Many homeowners accept the first check and move on without asking questions, but a careful review of your estimate can uncover repairs and held-back funds that were left out.
